Five ways this app could look

Each concept below is a complete visual world applied to all seventeen surfaces of To-Do-List — the same tasks, the same strings, the same states, so what differs is the world and not the content. Pick one and we go into detail on it; nothing here is committed.

The six accept / edit / reject frames are the ones to look at hardest. Your instructor's feedback on the product brief asks for exactly that, in those words: «Vis i UX-steget hvordan godta/endre/avvis ser ut på mobil og PC» — so every concept draws that flow at 360 px and at 1280 px, in three states each: all three parts proposed, one part being edited, and one part accepted with its undo still live.

Three things no concept may decide for you

  • Interface language The PRD fixes task text as English only and calls the interface language a separate question it does not answer. Every concept renders English strings as a working assumption. Confirm or change it.
  • Accessibility standard Nothing is named anywhere beyond NFR-4's 360 px and 1280 px. WCAG 2.2 AA is a reasonable floor; the concepts were held to 4.5:1 body contrast regardless. Decide whether AA is the committed bar.
  • Colour-only encoding All five concepts carry priority and engine provenance in a mark, shape or label as well as colour, on the assumption that colour alone is not enough. Confirm it, because it constrains the palette of whichever concept wins.

Thesis

A task is a record with a history, not a row that blinks out of existence. This concept refuses the category's disappearing list — the item that fades on completion, the suggestion chip that vanishes when dismissed, the undo that lives four seconds in a toast and then is gone. Here every act leaves a dated mark on the slip: a part you accepted is stamped with the day you accepted it, a part you struck stays legible with a line through it, a completed task keeps every mark it earned. The product asks you to trust a list you did not sort. Trust comes from being able to see what happened to it.

Signature move

Every state is stamped, dated, and never removed. Accepting a part strikes SET 16 NOV across it in set-blue and turns its value blue (m4, m5, d3, d4, d5). Rejecting strikes the value through and leaves it readable with its reason beneath. Past due is the OVERDUE strike in vermilion, rotated off square, sitting on the row itself (m1, m7, d1). A pending suggestion is an unfilled PENDING outline — the one mark that also appears offline (m2). Completing keeps every mark; the archive is the card pocket, not a wastebasket (d6).

Three disciplines carried in from directions this one beat: Live readout — the apparatus states its own numbers in Oswald caps rather than hiding them: counts per list, the reference date, what has been stamped and struck, and in Settings the fact that the Fixture has not been scored yet. Nothing disappears, it cancels — every removal is a mark, not an absence. The pending queue clears in place — on the phone, reviewing advances without navigating.

Accept / edit / reject

Each part is its own bordered block carrying the part name, the engine that proposed it, the proposed value, and three equal buttons: ACCEPT EDIT REJECT. Same size, same weight, always visible, never behind a disclosure. Accept all / Reject all sit apart below a rule, in plain outline, under the line "Shortcuts. They do nothing the three blocks above cannot do one at a time." The dominant arrangement on the screen is per-part action, which is what the PRD's counter-metric against an acceptance-rate-optimised UI actually requires.

Accepting stamps the block, turns the value blue, and puts a real UNDO button in the block — one step, restoring the previous value (m5, d4). Editing replaces the value with a standard segmented control and SAVE / CANCEL, and writes your pick rather than the proposal (m4, d3). Rejecting strikes the value and notes that it will not be proposed again for this task.

Priority is a stack of three bars — filled by level — beside its word, so it survives without colour. Engine provenance is a boxed R or M plus the name spelled out. Past due carries the word OVERDUE, not just vermilion. Nothing in this concept is encoded by colour alone.

360 and 1280

At 360 the slip is a single column: record at the top, three part-blocks stacked, shortcuts beneath, and a standard bottom tab bar. At 1280 the shell becomes a left nav rail of derived lists and a right apparatus column — the live readout that has no room on the phone. The part-blocks widen rather than multiply, so the review reads identically at both widths while the desktop gains the running commentary: what is stamped, what is struck, what is still open, what has left the device. The phone drops the apparatus column, not the controls.

Honest risk

Kraft is the hardest ground in this round to keep accessible: vermilion small text fails 4.5:1 on it unless darkened, which is why this concept carries two vermilions, and any later palette drift silently breaks contrast. The stamped history is also a bet that accumulation reads as trust rather than as clutter — on a task reviewed, edited and re-suggested several times, the marks will pile up, and the concept has no answer yet for the slip that gets too busy. And a library card is a nostalgic object: it risks reading as decoration to someone who wants a fast list, which is exactly the user this product has.

Thesis

A suggestion is not a chip row under a task. It is a set of marks in the margin, each one bracketed back to the exact words that caused it, waiting to be set, amended or struck. The mechanism is a proofreader's galley: two hands, two inks — the reader who marks, the author who disposes. That gives the product the one thing a chip row cannot: the user can see why each part was proposed, because the lead line points at the evidence. What it refuses is the category's edge-to-edge list with suggestions stacked beneath it in the same measure. Here the task block is held deliberately short and the review gets real margin beside it.

Signature move

Marks bracketed to their span. On d2–d4, hairline rules sit under *assignment* and *IBE160* and lead out to the Tags mark; a rule under *Submit* leads to Priority; a bracket pair embracing the whole line leads to Summary. The lead takes the mark's state colour, so a glance reads which parts are live. The same brackets appear in miniature on m9 as the first-run legend, on m6/d5 as the blue bracket on each set field, and as margin braces grouping Smart List rows on d1, d6, d7, d8.

Accept / edit / reject

Each mark is its own block carrying part name, engine, the bracketed span, the proposed value, and three equal standard buttons: Accept, Edit, Reject — same size, same weight, always visible, never behind a disclosure (m3, d2). Accept all / Reject all sit apart, in outline, at the foot of the margin, labelled as shortcuts. Edit opens a standard segmented control in place (m4, d3) and writes your pick, not the proposal. An accepted mark turns blue, is set into the line's field table, and offers Undo with a drawn arrow (m5, d4). A struck mark stays in the margin in graphite with a strike through it, and the field's note says the old value stands. The field table above the marks shows the three current values at all times, so "accepting writes only that part" is legible rather than promised. Priority carries a drawn chevron mark plus its word; engine provenance carries a drawn table glyph (Rule) or star glyph (Model) plus its name — neither is colour-only. Past due uses a diamond and the words "past due", not vermilion, which belongs to proposals alone.

360 and 1280

At 360 there is no margin, so the bracket rotates. A 28px stem gutter runs down the left of the sheet; each span's rule drops into its own lane in that gutter and turns in to its mark below, and each mark re-sets its span as a quoted fragment under the same hairline rule — so the connection survives even if the eye loses the lead. Marks stack full width beneath the line in part order. At 1280 the leads turn right into true margin and the marks sit beside the sentence. Same apparatus, rotated 90°, not a reflow: the phone version is a column proof, the desktop version a galley.

Honest risk

This is the closest of the five to what any model ships for a document-ish tool: warm ground, text serif, red accent, editorial register. That destination is well worn and a reviewer may read it as tasteful default rather than a decision. What earns it is load-bearing: the red is not an accent, it is a state with one meaning; the brackets are real geometry pointing at real spans, which is the rubric's "show why" requirement drawn rather than described. The genuine cost is upkeep — every new field needs a span to bracket to, and a suggestion whose cause is diffuse (Figure out the thing) has nothing to point at, so the apparatus must degrade gracefully. And the wide margin spends desktop space that a denser product would fill with tasks.

Thesis

On an orienteering map the course layer never alters the terrain beneath it: the purple is printed over fixed ink and changes nothing it crosses. That is already this product's central promise, so the concept makes it literal. The user's sentence and the fields as they stand are terrain, in dark ink. A suggestion arrives as a purple layer laid over that record — offset, overlapping its edge, visibly a second pass — and edits nothing until a part is punched. It refuses the category default where an assistant's values arrive pre-filled in the fields with an undo offered afterwards, and the related default of one dominant Accept all with per-part actions folded behind a disclosure.

Signature move

Punching the controls. The proposal is a purple-ruled layer over the record, offset right and down, overlapping its corner, carrying three numbered controls — 1 Tags, 2 Priority, 3 Summary — joined by purple legs, with a dashed leg run from the sentence to control 1. Accepting *punches* it: the circle fills with ink, pin-prick marks appear, and the value settles into the record above in the terrain's own ink while the purple leaves that part. Rejecting strikes the circle through. A purple start triangle marks any task with a control still open. It lives on m2–m6 and d2–d5; m9 and d1 carry the unpunched three-control legend instead.

Accept / edit / reject

Every control carries its own Accept / Edit / Reject as three equal bordered 11px buttons — mobile stacks the controls, each with its own row of three; desktop sets the three controls side by side so all nine actions are visible at once. Accept all / Reject all sit below the panel as smaller, lighter outline buttons captioned "Shortcuts. They do nothing the three controls cannot", so the per-part path is three times more present than the shortcut. Edit opens a standard segmented control (low / medium / high) with Apply / Cancel and states that your value is written, not the proposal. Undo is a real bordered button, on the punched control and beside the settled field (m5, d4). Priority is a three-bar ladder plus the word; provenance is a drawn mark plus "RULE ENGINE" spelled out — neither is encoded by colour alone.

360 and 1280

Mobile runs the legs vertically: one purple column offset 14px right of the record, controls read in sequence, the list below in banded groups under a tab bar. Desktop turns the legs horizontal: the record becomes a wide field table and the course panel sits to its right, overlapping its corner, with the three controls as columns — one glance instead of three. The list becomes a true control description sheet (task / tags / priority / due) beside a rail of Smart Lists and tag counts, with a week ladder aside. The review panel is composed for each width, not reflowed.

Honest risk

Purple is the strongest thing here and it is absent exactly where the user spends most of their time. On m1, d1, m7, d6 and Settings there is no pending suggestion and therefore no purple at all: those screens are carried by the inks and the density alone — the yellow window band, the olive thicket band, marsh rows in the Archive, brown rules, tabular columns. I think that holds, and it is why the Smart List groups are banded rather than faintly tinted. But it is a quieter world than the review surface, and whoever builds this will be tempted to borrow purple for a selected row or a primary button, which destroys the single reservation the whole palette depends on. Second cost: the tinted grounds are legible but unforgiving. Each small-type pair had to be checked by hand, and the next contributor who adds an unverified tint will break contrast before they notice it.

Interface strings are English as the working assumption; the PRD leaves interface language open and this concept does not settle it.

Thesis

A reference edition drawn by a ruling engine: short-measure columns of roman type held small inside a wide annotation margin, twinned around a hairline channel that carries the suggestion's three parts as square detents. Every state is a printed mark, never a colour wash. It refuses the category arrangement — a full-bleed list that grows to the viewport, state told by coloured pills, and one dominant Accept button — and it refuses that arrangement's quieter cousin, a centred max-width column with dead grey gutters.

Signature move

The centre rail. A 1px vertical channel with square detents cut into it wherever the flow changes footing, and letter-keys printed on the rail above each detent. Hairline square at rest; solid vermilion when pressed; bracketed on focus; struck with a bar when rejected or spent; run through with a rule when unavailable. It carries T / P / S on m2–m5 and d2–d4, the task's accepted fields on m6 and d5, the two engines as R / M on m8, d7, d8, and footing cuts alone on the list frames. The full key is printed in d2's margin and below m3's content.

Accept / edit / reject

Each part is a band across both columns: left is what the record holds now, right is what the engine proposes. Three identical buttons — ACCEPT, EDIT, REJECT, 96px, same border, no fill, no primary — sit under each proposal. Accept All / Reject All are underlined text links below the last band, carrying the line "shortcuts — they do nothing the three rows above cannot". Accepting fills the detent and brackets the value out of the proposal column into the record column; the right side then reads ACCEPTED beside a live UNDO and "single step — restores the previous value". Rejecting strikes the proposal and prints "unchanged — the rejection wrote nothing". Editing opens a standard select, drawn open with high chosen, APPLY / CANCEL, and "editing writes your value, not the proposal". d3/m4 show the other two parts untouched.

Because the vocabulary is printed marks, the product's open requirement is met structurally rather than by addition: priority is a bottom-aligned stack of 1px rules — three, two, one — plus the word; provenance is the engine named in words plus a drawn mark (Rule Engine a ruled square, Model Engine a square crossed off-axis); selection and the active nav item take a filled square as well as the rubric. Colour never carries any of them alone.

360 and 1280

Not a reflow. At 1280 the rail runs *between* the columns and is read across: record left, proposal right, one part per band. At 360 there is one column, the rail moves outboard down the left edge, the two columns collapse into a vertical order — current value right-aligned on the label row, proposal beneath, buttons beneath that — and the apparatus that lived in the margin moves *below* the content: the state key, the engine line, the counts. Buttons are 90 × 32 and sit clear of the bottom nav, so one part's accept/edit/reject is one thumb reach, with no horizontal scroll at either width. Motion, where it exists, is the rail: one damped axis, no overshoot.

Honest risk

Three values and no colour coding costs real glanceability on a product whose four tag contexts and three priority levels all want telling apart at speed. Marks and position carry priority well — the rule-stack is scannable down a column and never ambiguous — and they carry part-state and provenance very well, because those are few and always labelled. Tags are where it strains: school / group / work / personal are separated by their word alone, in 10px caps inside brackets, so a twenty-task list is read rather than glanced. A four-mark tag alphabet was considered and rejected as unlearnable; the honest mitigation is the per-tag Smart Lists in the nav, which is navigation, not recognition. Second cost: hairlines and 9px letterspaced apparatus are unforgiving. A half-pixel misalignment shows, and this density needs production discipline that a one-person course project may not have time for.

Thesis

Everything derived is on screen at once. This concept refuses the arrangement every task app in the category shares — one list in the middle, everything else folded behind a hamburger, a filter menu or a detail drawer — on the grounds that this product's whole promise is *views you never built*. Hiding nine automatically derived lists behind navigation hides the feature. At 1280 px the capture field, all three Smart Lists, all four tag lists, the archive, the untagged case, the mark legend and the pending suggestion are visible together, without scrolling and without a click. The density is not a style; it is the argument.

Signature move

Every module wears a small red tab naming it, and the pending suggestion opens in place as one module whose three parts are three rows of one ruled table — PART · PROPOSED VALUE · FIELD NOW · ACTION ON THIS PART ONLY. Nothing ever navigates away to review a suggestion. Directly beneath it sits the TASK NOW module, which prints what is actually written to the task at this instant, so the user can read the proposal and its consequence as two adjacent tables rather than holding one in memory. A MARKS module publishes the legend for every non-colour mark the mosaic uses.

Accept / edit / reject

The review is a table with one row per part, and each row carries its own ✓ Accept, ✎ Edit, ✕ Reject in its own cell. Per-part action is therefore the default and dominant arrangement, not a disclosure behind a primary button — the product's counter-metric against a UI that makes Accept the easy path is satisfied by the structure itself rather than by restraint. Accept all and Reject all are modest controls set below the table, labelled as shortcuts that do nothing the three rows above cannot. A module headed WHY ACCEPT IS NOT THE EASY PATH states this in the product's own words.

Accepting a part turns its row blue, writes only that field in TASK NOW, and swaps that row's control for a single-step ↺ Undo that restores the previous value. Rejecting strikes the row and leaves FIELD NOW untouched. Editing opens the chooser in the row itself with Apply / Cancel. Three different part-states are legible simultaneously in one table.

Priority is carried by a three/two/one bar mark as well as its word; engine provenance is carried by a named label (Rule Engine, Model Engine) plus its own mark. Neither is ever colour alone, and the MARKS module is where both are published.

360 and 1280

At 1280 the mosaic is four columns and everything derived is co-visible. At 360 it becomes one column in a fixed reading order — capture, then Need Attention, then the pending suggestion, then the due window — and the second nav row becomes a horizontal strip of count chips. What gives at 360 is co-visibility, deliberately: the tag lists and archive drop below the fold rather than shrinking further, because shrinking the type is what would actually break it. The tab system, the hairline rules and the 11–13 px body all survive the move; the module is the unit at both widths.

Honest risk

The type is small and this is the only concept where that is load-bearing. It is a genuine accessibility exposure on a phone held one-handed between classes, and it leaves no headroom: any later demand for a larger base size costs the co-visibility that is the concept's entire argument. The red tabs also risk reading as alarm to a first-time user before they learn the tab is just a label — and the grader meets this interface cold, once.

The quiet door — always available, never recommended

The familiar road

The category standard, played straight: a white card list with a left checkbox, a floating compose button, pastel pill tags, a right-side detail drawer, the platform system font. Todoist, TickTick and Things all live here, and it is a legitimate destination — it is what a grader will recognise instantly, and familiar-and-effective is not a failure of nerve. It is listed because the choice should be yours rather than mine. The cost is that the design criterion rewards et gjennomarbeidet design, and a competent copy of the category default is the one thing in this round that cannot be told apart from what any other student would ship.

What happens when you pick one. The chosen world gets written down as a direction contract, then DESIGN.md and EXPERIENCE.md close around it — the information architecture, the state vocabulary, the microcopy, the accessibility floor and the four user journeys. Both files currently hold frontmatter only, on purpose.

You can also ask for a fresh hand of five instead, steered safer (closer to what a grader recognises) or bolder (foreign forms at full commitment), and every world already shown is eliminated from it.

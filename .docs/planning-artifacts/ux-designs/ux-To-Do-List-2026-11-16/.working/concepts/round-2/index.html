Thesis

Your sentence and the machine's proposal stay two separate objects on the screen, and the proposal is laid over the record rather than mixed into it. That separation is the product's central promise made visible: the course layer never edits the terrain, which is the same rule as the app never rewriting what you typed. This concept refuses the arrangement where a suggestion arrives as an inline chip row inside the task — because once the proposal sits inside the record, nothing on screen says it is not yours yet.

It is built from your decisions. Kartet supplies the whole visual world. The borrowed frames keep their skeletons: Date Due's sectioned list and settings page (m1, m8), its three-part-block review with undo (m5); Tett's module structure for capture, task detail and lists (m2, m6, m7) and its full mosaic, reviews and settings on desktop (d1, d2, d4, d7, d8); Kartet's own review and archive (m3, m4, d3, d5, d6). What the imports lose is their state vocabulary — Date Due's rubber stamp and Tett's red tab are gone, replaced by punching a numbered control. One mechanic owns every frame.

Signature move

Punching the controls, on every surface. The proposal is a purple-ruled panel offset from the record, carrying three numbered controls joined by purple legs — 1 Tags, 2 Priority, 3 Summary — with a dashed leg from the record to control 1. Accepting punches the control: the circle fills with ink, pin-pricks appear, the value settles into the record in the terrain's own ink, and the purple leaves that part. Rejecting strikes the circle through and leaves it readable. A start triangle marks any task with a control still open.

The imports inherit it rather than keeping their own marks. Date Due's dated stamp becomes a punched control carrying its date (m5, d5); Tett's review table carries control numerals and punch glyphs in its first column (d2, d4); the task detail's history reads as a control description (m6, d5).

Accept / edit / reject

Each control is its own block, carrying the part name, the engine that proposed it, the proposed value, and three equal buttons: Accept Edit Reject. Same size, same weight, always visible, never behind a disclosure. Punch all / Strike all sit below a rule in plain outline, under the line "shortcuts · they do nothing the three controls cannot".

Punching turns the value to terrain ink, stamps it punched 16 nov, and puts a real Undo button in the block — one step, restoring the previous value (m5, d4). Editing replaces the value with a standard segmented control plus Apply / Cancel, and writes your pick rather than the proposal (m4, d3). Striking leaves the field untouched and notes that the control is not proposed again. A status row under the sheet shows all three control-states at a glance.

Priority is a three-bar ladder beside its word; engine provenance is a boxed R or M plus the name spelled out; past due carries the word as well as the olive. Nothing is encoded by colour alone, and every frame publishes the legend that says so.

360 and 1280

At 360 the course panel sits below the record, full width, controls stacked and joined by a vertical purple leg. On the dedicated review screen at 1280 (d3) it sits beside the record, with a dashed leg crossing the gap — the terrain column stays a short measure and the panel takes the width it gains. The spatial relationship is the same at both widths: two objects, one laid over the other. What changes is the axis.

Inside the desktop mosaic (d2, d4) the course becomes a purple-ruled table module in the grid, one row per control, because that is the structure you picked from Tett for those two frames. It is the same mechanic in a different container: the controls are still numbered, still punched, still acted on one at a time, and the In the record now module beside it shows what each punch actually wrote.

Honest risk

Two objects cost vertical space, and the phone pays. The review surface is the fullest screen in the app at 360, and a fourth part would not fit without scrolling the panel away from the record it belongs to — which is the one thing this concept cannot afford. The panel also goes quiet on every surface with no proposal open: on m1, m7 and d6 the purple simply is not there, so the world's most distinctive device is absent from roughly half the app, and those frames lean on the terrain inks alone to stay recognisable.

Thesis

One sheet, read across the row. Orienteering has a second artefact besides the map — the control description sheet, a dense coded table that tells you what each control actually is — and this concept makes that the spine instead of the map overlay. "What the field is now" and "what is proposed" sit on the same line, separated by an arrow, so the comparison is a glance rather than a look back and forth between two objects. The proposal is a column in the record's own sheet, marked by a purple rule down the row, not a layer floating beside it.

It is built from your decisions, with the same frame assignments as its sibling: Date Due's sectioned list and settings (m1, m8) and its three-states-at-once review (m5); Tett's structure for capture, task detail and lists (m2, m6, m7) and its desktop mosaic and settings (d1, d2, d4, d7, d8); Kartet's review, task detail and archive (m3, m4, d3, d5, d6). Because the sheet is Tett's table rendered in Kartet's ink, d2 and d4 are the same structure here — your choice of Tett for d4 needed no change in this concept. The difference from 06 Kurs is where the merge resolves: here Tett's density is native rather than imported, and Date Due's dated marks survive literally, as dated entries in a description column (punched Mon 16 Nov 09:14 · rule engine) rather than being translated into a stamp.

Signature move

The proposal is a column, and the row is read across. Every reviewable part is one row of the control description sheet: # · control · in the record now · proposed · description · act. The description column is the concept's own invention and does real work — it carries the dated history (punched Mon 16 Nov 09:14), the reason a struck row will not return, and what applying an edit will write. An open row wears a purple left rule and a purple proposed value; punching turns the value to terrain ink and rewrites the description with its date; striking rules the proposal through and leaves the row in place.

The same sheet grammar runs the whole app: task detail is a field sheet plus a control-description sheet (m6, d5), the archive is a sheet (d6), the lists are sheets with ruled column heads (m1, d1), and Settings is a stack of sheets (m8, d7, d8).

Accept / edit / reject

Each row carries its own Accept Edit Reject in its own act column — per-row action is the default and dominant arrangement, not a disclosure. Punch all / Strike all sit below the sheet as plain outline buttons labelled as shortcuts that do nothing the three rows cannot do one at a time. A dedicated panel, Why Accept is not the easy path, states this in the product's own words on d2, d3 and d4.

Punching puts a real Undo in the act column — one step, restoring the previous value. Editing opens a standard segmented control in the row itself with Apply / Cancel. Striking rules the proposed value through and the description says it will not be re-proposed. All three states are legible on one sheet at once (m5, d4).

Priority is a bar ladder beside its word, provenance is a boxed initial plus the spelled-out engine name, past due carries the words "past due". Never colour alone, and the legend is published on the frame.

360 and 1280

At 1280 a row really is one line: six columns, read straight across. At 360 a four-column sheet cannot hold a real task sentence, so the row takes two lines and keeps its columns — the sentence on the first, the coded values on the second, with the in the record → proposed pair sharing one line under the control's name. The rules, the column heads and the act column survive the move; only the wrap changes. This is the concept's real responsive answer, and the place it is most exposed.

Honest risk

Density is the whole argument, and it is also the liability. At 1280 this concept puts more on screen than any other in either round; at 360 that advantage inverts into two-line rows that are taller than Kurs's, so the phone pays for the desktop's win. The description column is powerful and also the easiest thing in the app to let rot — it is prose in a table, and prose in a table is what nobody updates. And the purple-as-a-rule decision is quieter than a purple panel: it respects the sheet, but a hurried user scanning a dense screen may miss that a row is still open, which is exactly the mistake this product cannot afford.
